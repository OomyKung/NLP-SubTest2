WAN Concepts (Wide Area Networks)

A Wide Area Network (WAN) connects LANs that are far apart, such as a head office and its branch offices in different cities. A LAN is owned by the organization and covers a small area, while a WAN crosses a large area using links rented from a service provider. A private WAN is a dedicated connection from a provider, while a public WAN uses the internet, often protected with a VPN.

WAN terms and definitions:
- CPE (Customer Premises Equipment): devices at the customer site.
- DTE (Data Terminal Equipment): the customer device that sends data, usually the router.
- DCE (Data Communications Equipment): connects the DTE to the provider and provides clocking, such as a modem or CSU/DSU.
- Demarcation point: the point where customer responsibility ends and provider responsibility begins.
- Local loop (last mile): the cable from the demarcation point to the provider CO.
- Central office (CO): the provider building that connects customers to the provider network.
- Toll network: the long-distance links and devices inside the provider network.
- POP (Point of Presence): a provider location where customers connect to the provider network.

WAN topologies:
- Point-to-point: one permanent link between two sites.
- Hub-and-spoke: branch sites (spokes) connect to a central hub. Spoke-to-spoke traffic goes through the hub.
- Dual-homed: a site connects to two hubs or two provider routers for redundancy.
- Fully meshed: every site connects to every other site, needing n(n-1)/2 links.
- Partially meshed: only the important sites are connected to each other.

How data crosses a WAN, step by step:
1. A host sends a packet to its default gateway, the branch edge router.
2. The router encapsulates the packet in a WAN Layer 2 frame, such as HDLC, PPP, or Ethernet.
3. The frame crosses the DCE and the local loop to the provider CO.
4. The provider moves the data across its toll network to the CO near the remote site.
5. The remote edge router removes the WAN frame and forwards the packet to its LAN.

Traditional WAN connectivity:
- Leased lines: a dedicated point-to-point circuit. A T1 runs at 1.544 Mbps (North America) and an E1 runs at 2.048 Mbps (Europe).
- Circuit-switched: a circuit is set up for each call, as in the PSTN (dialup) and ISDN.
- Packet-switched: many customers share the provider network. Frame Relay and ATM are packet-switched technologies that are now legacy.
- Optical: SONET and SDH are fiber transport standards, and DWDM sends many wavelengths of light over one fiber.

Modern WAN connectivity:
- Metro Ethernet (Ethernet WAN): the provider extends Ethernet across a city, so the customer uses a normal Ethernet interface.
- MPLS (Multiprotocol Label Switching): provider routers forward packets using short labels instead of IP lookups. MPLS can carry any protocol, such as IPv4, IPv6, and Ethernet, and it supports QoS.

Internet-based WAN connectivity:
- DSL: always-on service over the telephone line. ADSL is asymmetric, with faster download than upload. A DSL modem at the customer connects to a DSLAM at the provider CO, and speed drops as the distance to the CO grows.
- Cable: internet over the cable TV network. It uses the DOCSIS standard, a cable modem at the customer, a CMTS at the provider headend, and a hybrid fiber-coaxial (HFC) network.
- Fiber: FTTH (fiber to the home), FTTB (fiber to the building), and FTTN (fiber to the node or neighborhood).
- Wireless: municipal Wi-Fi, cellular 4G and 5G, and satellite (high latency) for remote areas.
- VPN over the internet: a cheap, secure way to connect sites.

WAN serial link configuration example:
On a serial link the DCE side must provide the clock. The default encapsulation on a Cisco serial interface is HDLC.
Router(config)# interface serial 0/0/0
Router(config-if)# ip address 10.0.0.1 255.255.255.252
Router(config-if)# clock rate 64000
Router(config-if)# no shutdown

WAN verification:
Router# show controllers serial 0/0/0
Interface Serial0/0/0
Hardware is PowerQUICC MPC860
DCE V.35, clock rate 64000
The output shows whether the cable end is DCE or DTE and the clock rate. "show interfaces serial 0/0/0" shows the encapsulation and line protocol status.

WAN ISP connectivity options:
- Single-homed: one connection to one ISP. It is cheap but has no redundancy.
- Dual-homed: two connections to the same ISP.
- Multihomed: connections to two or more different ISPs.
- Dual-multihomed: two connections to each of two or more ISPs, the most redundant option.

WAN troubleshooting:
A serial link that stays down often has no clock rate on the DCE side, mismatched encapsulation, or a cable problem. Slow DSL can mean a long distance to the DSLAM.

WAN comparison, private WAN vs internet-based WAN:
- Private WAN (leased line, Metro Ethernet, MPLS): predictable performance with service level agreements, but higher cost.
- Internet-based WAN (DSL, cable, fiber, cellular): low cost, but no guaranteed performance, so a VPN adds security.

CCNA exam tips for WAN concepts:
- A T1 is 1.544 Mbps and an E1 is 2.048 Mbps.
- The DCE provides clocking; the DTE is usually the customer router.
- DSL uses a DSLAM, cable uses a CMTS and DOCSIS.

Common questions about WAN concepts:
Q: What is the demarcation point in a WAN?
A: It is the point where the customer network ends and the service provider network begins.

Q: What is the speed of a T1 and an E1 line?
A: A T1 line runs at 1.544 Mbps and an E1 line runs at 2.048 Mbps.

Q: What is the difference between DTE and DCE?
A: The DTE is the customer device that sends data, usually a router, and the DCE connects it to the provider and provides the clock.

Q: What is a hub-and-spoke WAN topology?
A: Branch sites connect only to a central hub site, so spoke-to-spoke traffic passes through the hub.

Q: What is the difference between dual-homed and multihomed?
A: Dual-homed means two connections to the same ISP, while multihomed means connections to two or more different ISPs.
